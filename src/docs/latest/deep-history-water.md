# The Drain at the Bottom of the Bath

*The cities of the Sindhu-Sarasvatī spent their largest engineering on water, and "sanitation" is a word that arrives four thousand years too late to name what they were doing.*

In the winter of 2024 the Archaeological Survey opened ground between two of the mounds at Rakhigarhi and found a reservoir - the largest yet identified anywhere in the Sindhu-Sarasvatī world outside Dholavira. Running past it was the dry bed of the Chautang, which is the Dṛṣadvatī of the Ṛgveda. The find went around the wires in the register these things always get. Ingenious water management. Advanced for its time. Lessons for a water-stressed India. A five-thousand-year-old civilization was congratulated on its plumbing.

The plumbing deserves the congratulation. It is the framing that deserves suspicion, because the framing has been doing quiet work for a century, and the work it does is to make the most conspicuous fact about these cities disappear into a compliment.

Consider what the fact actually is. Michael Jansen, after years of survey at Mohenjo-daro, put the number of wells in that city at something above seven hundred, sunk into a settlement that may have held forty thousand people. The wells required a brick shaped for no other purpose - a wedge, tapered so that a course of them would close into a true cylinder without mortar failing under the water table. Someone designed a specialist brick so that a city could have a well on nearly every block. Inside the houses, almost every excavated dwelling has a bathing floor: brick set on edge and fitted so tightly that the surface is close to watertight, sloped to one corner, drained through the house wall into a channel running under the street. The street channels are covered and have inspection openings. And on the citadel mound sits the one building this civilization built that could be called a monument: a tank twelve metres by seven and two and a half deep, its floor and walls made of two skins of sawn brick laid in gypsum mortar with a membrane of bitumen sandwiched between them, a second wall behind that packed with more bitumen, a well in an adjoining room to fill it, staircases descending into it at either end, and a corbelled drain cut through one corner to empty it out over the western side of the mound.

At Dholavira, out on the Rann where no river runs at all, they threw dams across two seasonal streams and cut sixteen reservoirs into the rock inside the walls, the largest of them around eighty metres long, holding among them a quantity of water it is easier to state in hundreds of thousands of cubic metres than to picture. In a place where there was no water, they built a city whose largest structures are the places where water is kept.

Set all of this beside what the same civilization did not build. No palace has been found at any of its two thousand settlements. No royal tomb. No temple that anybody has been able to identify with confidence as one. The largest, costliest, most carefully engineered things these people made are containers for water that are also designed to let the water go.

## The word that arrives too late

Marshall found the tank and called it a bath. Wheeler found the drains and called them a sanitation system. The sentence that has come down into every Indian schoolbook since - that the Harappans had drainage superior to many modern Indian towns - is half compliment and half insult and entirely beside the point. It reads a Bronze Age city as an early attempt at municipal engineering, scores it against a Victorian benchmark, and awards marks.

The trouble is that "sanitation" is a nineteenth-century European manufacture with a datable birth, an idea young enough to have a paper trail. Chadwick's sanitary report is 1842; the Public Health Act is 1848; miasma gives way to germ theory across the following four decades. Only after that sequence does cleanliness become a technical matter of removing pathogenic material from surfaces, separable in principle from any question of what a person is or what state they are in. Before that sequence, in Europe as everywhere else on earth, washing was a moral and religious act that happened also to remove dirt.

So the question the literature keeps asking - was the Great Bath ritual or was it sanitary - requires a Bronze Age city to sort itself into two bins that were fabricated in London around 1845. The city cannot answer, because the city did not have the bins. The more recent scholarly move, that the two purposes "need not be mutually exclusive," is generous and still wrong in the same way: it keeps the categories and softens the boundary between them, when the categories are the artefact.

The question worth asking is what kind of purity these people were pursuing, and whether brick can tell us.

## The drain

It can, and the piece of brick that tells us is the drain.

Think about what it costs to build the Great Bath. Two skins of sawn brick. Gypsum mortar. A continuous bitumen membrane. A second wall behind the first with more bitumen packed into the cavity. This is an enormous expenditure of skill and material aimed at a single outcome: the tank must hold water and lose none of it. Every hour of that labour argues for keeping the water in.

And then they cut a corbelled drain through the corner and threw it all away.

##### The bitumen says they wanted the water to stay. The drain says they wanted it gone. Both were paid for, and the second cost more, because it meant the first had to be done again.

If the object were the removal of soil from bodies, the sensible design is a tank you keep full. Filling is the expensive operation - here, drawing by hand from a well and carrying it. A civilization optimizing for washing would have built a basin and topped it up. Instead they built a vessel of extraordinary integrity and then guaranteed that it would be emptied.

There is only one reason to do that, and the tradition descended from these cities has never stopped observing it: water that has been used is no longer water that purifies. Purification requires water that is flowing, or water that is renewed. Standing water does not do the work. Every temple tank in India is fed and drained; every ghat is a set of steps into a current; the immersion at the close of a rite happens in a river or in something arranged to behave like one. The drain in the corner of the Great Bath is the earliest surviving statement of that rule, made in brick and bitumen by people who could not yet write anything we can read.

## Śuci

The vocabulary confirms what the drain implies, and confirms it from a direction the drain could not reach.

The Sanskrit word for the pure is śuci, from the root √śuc, which means to blaze, to gleam, to shine. Śauca, the state or discipline of purity, is that same gleam held as a condition of the person. Agni is called śuci constantly in the Ṛgveda; fire is the standard case of the pure, because fire shines and because nothing that enters it comes out as what it was.

The etymology settles the question. In this civilization's own vocabulary, purity is a positive property - radiance - held by the person the way heat is held by iron. Dirt is not what purity is measured against. Dullness is.

Which is why the operation is pouring and immersion rather than scrubbing. Scrubbing subtracts; immersion puts you in contact with a substance that is itself śuci and lets the property transfer. Water and fire are the two great purifiers of the tradition for the same reason, and the yajña sits between them: fire at the centre, water at both ends. A hygienic culture, given four thousand years, invents soap. This one invented the stepwell.

## One bathing floor per house

The argument does not actually rest on the Great Bath, which is a single building and can always be explained away as the eccentricity of a ruling group nobody has found. It rests on the ordinary house, and there it is much harder to dismiss.

Nearly every excavated dwelling at Mohenjo-daro has its own bathing floor. Not the large houses; the houses. Each one drains through the wall into the lane, which means the covered channels beneath the streets were sized on the assumption that every household in the city would put water into them every day. The public works are downstream of a private habit. Somebody laid out those lanes knowing in advance what the people who would live along them were going to do each morning.

No Bronze Age state anywhere mandated daily bathing by its whole population. None could have. There was no mechanism, and there is no sign anyone tried. Which leaves the only remaining explanation: it was already the custom, older than the city, and the city was built to accommodate it.

Compare the alternatives. Knossos has drains and it has lustral basins. Amarna has bathing rooms. In both cases the provision is for palaces and for the villas of officials - purity as a prerogative of rank, administered from above, performed on behalf of the collective by those entitled to perform it. In the Sindhu-Sarasvatī cities the provision is domestic and it is universal. Every householder purifies himself, in his own house, with water he draws himself, and the street merely carries away what he has already done.

This is the same signature the archaeology of these cities gives everywhere - order carried inside the social body rather than imposed on it from a throne - and it is the reason the discipline keeps calling this civilization faceless. It is looking for the authority that enforces the pattern. There is no such authority. There is a household that knows what it owes, and a city built around the aggregate of a few thousand households all knowing the same thing.

## Fire between two rivers

Now open the text that the people of these cities left, and see whether it recognizes the place.

In the third maṇḍala of the Ṛgveda, Agni is set down and told to burn: *dṛṣadvatyāṃ mānuṣa āpayāyāṃ sarasvatyāṃ revad agne didīhi.* On the Dṛṣadvatī, at Mānuṣa, on the Āpayā, on the Sarasvatī - shine richly. The fire is kindled by naming the rivers it stands among. And the Dṛṣadvatī is the stream whose dry bed runs past the reservoir the Survey opened at Rakhigarhi two winters ago.

The waters are addressed directly and often. *Apo hi ṣṭhā mayobhuvaḥ* - waters, you who make joy - a verse still recited at snāna by people who have never heard of Mohenjo-daro. Apāṃ Napāt, child of the waters, is a fire that lives in the flood, a deity in whom the two purifiers are one thing. And the great soma sacrifice is bracketed by water at both ends: the dīkṣā, where the yajamāna bathes and passes into the consecrated state, and the avabhṛtha at the close, where he and his wife and the implements of the rite go down into flowing water and the whole structure is dissolved back into the river. Fire is what the sacrifice does. Water is how it is entered and how it is put down.

None of this reads as the liturgy of newcomers describing a practice they have just met. It reads as the technical vocabulary of people who had been building tanks for a very long time.

## The crossing

There is one more piece of evidence, and it sits in the most ordinary word the tradition has.

A sacred place in India is a tīrtha, from √tṛ, to cross over. The word means a ford - a spot where the water is shallow enough to get to the other side. Every other civilization put its holiness on a height, or shut it inside a built house for the god. This one put it at the water's edge and named it for the crossing. Pilgrimage is tīrtha-yātrā, going to fords. The Jaina word for a teacher is Tīrthaṅkara, ford-maker, one who builds a place to get across. The whole metaphorics of liberation in Indian thought is a river-crossing, because the physical act underneath the metaphor was a river-crossing, performed daily, by everyone.

Read the Great Bath again with that word in hand. Steps down at the north end and steps down at the south. Water entering from a well and leaving through a drain, so that it moves. It is a ford with the river taken out of it - a crossing manufactured on a mound in a city whose great river was already beginning to fail.

## The coincidence nobody wants

The objection to all of this is obvious and should be stated plainly: I am reading the present into the past. Continuity is being assumed rather than shown.

So consider what the alternative reading has to carry. It must explain the drain in a tank built at ruinous expense to be watertight. It must explain a bathing floor in every ordinary house and a street system engineered around the assumption of daily use. It must explain why a city on the Rann with no river spent its wealth on sixteen reservoirs. And having explained all that, it must then accept that the most water-preoccupied Bronze Age civilization on earth was succeeded, on the same soil, by the most water-preoccupied living civilization on earth, and that the resemblance is an accident.

Because the resemblance is total. The tank at every temple. The kuṇḍa. The ghat. The stepwell at Modhera and the stepwell at Patan, which are Harappan reservoirs with three thousand more years of stonework on them. The daily snāna. The ācamana before any rite at all, three sips from the palm. The image carried down to the water and immersed at the end of a festival, which is the avabhṛtha under a later name. And sixty-six crore people walking into a confluence at Prayagraj across six weeks of 2025 to do the thing the guidebooks say the Great Bath's purpose is unknown.

That coincidence is only necessary if you have already decided there was a break. The break is what the Aryan theory requires. The ground has never produced it. Remove the requirement and the evidence relaxes into the reading it was always offering: the same people, the same practice, on the same rivers, for as long as there has been anyone there to perform it. The Sarasvatī failed around 1900 BCE and the population moved east, and the practice moved with them, because a practice that lives in the householder rather than in a temple or a bureaucracy is exactly the kind of thing that survives a migration. That is what distributed order is for.

Stand today at the north stair of the Great Bath and you will be told that the function of the structure remains uncertain. Away to the east, on a February morning, sixty-six crore people will walk down a flight of steps into a river, come up, and go home. One of these two things is a mystery. The tradition has never been in any doubt about which.
