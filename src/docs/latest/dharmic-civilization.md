# The Text With No Author

*Why the method that unmade European religious authority found nothing to cut in India, and where the wound was actually inflicted.*

The nineteenth century brought to India the sharpest instrument it had ever forged, and the instrument found almost nothing to cut.

The instrument was historical criticism. In Europe it had done extraordinary work. Within three generations it had taken the Pentateuch apart into documentary strands, dissolved the Gospels into layers of redaction and community memory, moved Moses from author to legend and Jesus from God to a Galilean preacher subsequently mythologised by Hellenistic converts. Strauss, Baur, Wellhausen. By 1880 an educated German could hold the Bible in his hands and see its seams. The confidence of an entire religious order did not survive the encounter.

The same men, in several cases literally the same men, turned to the Veda. Max Müller, editing the Rgveda with Sāyaṇa's commentary at Oxford, wrote privately that laying the root bare was the surest way to uproot everything that had grown from it over three thousand years. He was not being cynical. He was applying a method that had worked, and he expected it to work again. Weber, Whitney, Oldenberg, later Keith and Winternitz, stratified the hymns, dated the layers, traced the borrowings, mapped the interpolations. The apparatus was formidable and much of the technical work was excellent. The intended effect never arrived.

The standard explanation for this is condescending, and it is the one still taught. Indians were insufficiently modern. The critical spirit had not penetrated. Priestly authority survived because the peasantry could not read. This explanation has the great advantage of requiring no thought.

The real reason is structural. The critique was aimed with considerable precision at a load-bearing wall that does not exist in this building.

## What Historical Criticism Actually Attacks

Authority in the Abrahamic traditions is genealogical. God spoke, at a time, in a place, to a named man. The text is the record of that speech. The chain of custody running from the event to the record is what makes the text binding on anyone. Every link in that chain is a historical claim, and therefore every link is attackable by historical means. If Moses did not write the Torah, the covenant's paperwork is in question. If the resurrection narratives are late compositions of a community with motives, the resurrection is in question.

The method works because it drives a wedge between the text and its claimed origin, and the origin is where the authority lives. This is why the encounter was so violent in Europe. The scholarship went after provenance while leaving belief formally untouched, and provenance was structural.

##### A tradition whose authority runs through a person can be unmade by a biography. Historical criticism is a biography-dissolving machine, and it works on anything that has a founder.

Bring that machine to a tradition that has no founder and it idles.

## Why Jaimini Removed the Speaker

Purva Mimamsa holds that the Veda is apauruṣeya. Unauthored. Not composed by any person, and in the older Mimamsa not composed by Isvara either. To the modern ear this is the most transparent piece of priestly self-interest ever devised: declare your text uncreated and no one may question it. That reading treats the doctrine as a conclusion. It is a solution, and to see it you have to see the problem.

Jaimini opens by defining his subject. Dharma is the good characterised by injunction, *codanā-lakṣaṇo 'rtho dharmaḥ*. Dharma is that which is known through Vedic injunction and nowhere else. And it must be, because nothing else reaches it. You cannot perceive that the agnihotra yields its fruit; perception gives present objects to the senses, and the fruit is future and the connection imperceptible. You cannot infer it, since inference needs a previously observed invariable concomitance and nobody has observed this one. Comparison, presumption, non-apprehension, none of them arrive. Dharma has exactly one door.

So what makes a statement about dharma authoritative? Suppose a person spoke it. Then its authority rests on that person's reliability, and reliability must itself be established by perception or inference, which we have just agreed do not reach dharma. Postulate an omniscient author and the problem returns enlarged: to certify omniscience you need a criterion for omniscience, and that criterion has to come from somewhere. Every route that passes through a speaker generates a regress.

The Mimamsaka's exit is to delete the speaker. The relation between word and meaning is *autpattika*, inborn, never instituted by anyone at any point. Language is beginningless, the Veda is the eternal ordering within language, and śabda is therefore a pramāṇa in its own right rather than a derivative of perception. Kumārila supplies the second half with *svataḥ prāmāṇya*: cognitions carry their validity intrinsically, and it is invalidity that has to be established. A Vedic statement is authoritative on arrival and stays so until defeated. This inverts the modern evidentiary posture and is considerably more coherent than it first appears, since the alternative, that every cognition must be certified before it counts, is itself a regress of certifications.

One need not accept any of this. The Naiyayikas did not, and argued hard for a divine author. The Buddhists attacked the whole edifice with great skill, and Kumārila spent much of his working life answering them. Who won is a separate question. What concerns us here is what the doctrine does to the shape of the tradition that holds it.

It removes the person. And a tradition with no person at its origin offers no biography to dissolve.

## The Seers Were Never Authors

The ṛṣis are *mantra-draṣṭāraḥ*, seers of the mantras. The tradition's own account is that they saw what was already there, the way an astronomer sees a star instead of manufacturing one. Attribution of hymns to Vasistha, Visvamitra, Bharadvaja, the Angirasas, records custody and lineage. It is not a copyright claim, and it was never asked to bear the weight that the name Moses bears.

Which means the philologist who demonstrates that the ninth mandala is compositionally distinct from the second has demonstrated something the tradition never denied and never needed. The philologist who proves the Atharvan material was systematised later than the Rgvedic has proved a fact about transmission history, and transmission history was never the ground of the authority. Push the dating to 1500 BCE, or 1900, or 3000, and nothing doctrinal moves, because dharma was never staked on a date. There is no Sinai here to be shown empty. There is no tomb to be found occupied.

This accounts for a peculiar note of exasperation that runs through European Indology for two centuries and is usually misfiled as a complaint about Indian obscurantism. They kept producing devastating results, and the target kept failing to fall over.

The same structural fact explains something else that has generated a century and a half of official confusion. Without an author there is no orthodoxy in the doctrinal sense, and therefore no heresy in the doctrinal sense. The astika and nastika line falls in a place that makes no sense to a creedal reader. Mimamsa is astika and effectively non-theistic. Samkhya likewise. The Bauddha and Jaina darshanas are nastika while being more ascetically demanding than most of what they get contrasted against. The line is drawn at whether the Veda is accepted as pramāṇa, which is an epistemological commitment and not a proposition about the furniture of the universe. Handle Sanatana Dharma as a creed and you generate a category error every time, which is what the census officers have been doing since 1871 and the textbook committees since 1950.

## Where the Damage Was Actually Done

Now the harder part, and the part almost nobody was watching.

Consider what apauruṣeyatva demands operationally. If the Veda is the eternal ordering within language, then what matters is the sound, in its exact sequence, accent and quantity. A reconstructed meaning that an editor can defend in a footnote is beside the point, and no manuscript can be authoritative. The text lives in mouths.

And so this civilization built the most obsessive error-correcting technology any human society has ever built for anything. The vikṛti recitations, krama, jaṭā, ghana, permute the syllables against each other in fixed patterns so that a corruption introduced anywhere fails to reproduce itself consistently across the permutations and stands exposed. This is redundancy encoding, arrived at some three thousand years before Hamming, and it worked. Even the philologists who came to uproot conceded that the Rgveda had come across that span with a fidelity without parallel anywhere on earth.

An oral technology of that kind has a specific vulnerability, and the vulnerability is demographic and institutional. It requires an unbroken chain of households in which a boy begins at eight and a man is still reciting at seventy, and an economy that feeds him for doing it. Break the endowment, break the gurukula, break the temple economy, break the village that sustained the srotriya, and the chain parts. It parts quietly. There is no library burning to photograph. One generation simply does not begin.

Patanjali's Mahābhāṣya enumerates 1131 śākhās: twenty-one of the Rgveda, one hundred and one of the Yajurveda, a thousand of the Samaveda, nine of the Atharvaveda. These are recensions of the four, not separate Vedas, and the round thousand for the Samaveda is a traditional figure to be held loosely. Roughly a dozen survive today as texts. The count surviving as living recitation lineages, with the full vikṛti apparatus and unbroken transmission, is smaller. One śākhā of the Rgveda. A handful of the Yajurveda. Two of the Atharvaveda, one of which came within a generation or two of stopping.

That is the wound. Not the dating of the hymns. Colonial land settlement, the resumption of religious endowments, the redirection of every incentive in Indian society toward English education and government employment, and after 1947 a state that kept its hands on Hindu institutions while it released everyone else's, did what Weber's stratigraphy could not.

##### A tradition whose authority is impersonal cannot be touched at its origin and is acutely exposed at its continuity. The nineteenth century aimed at the first and did its real damage at the second, largely as a side effect.

We spent a hundred and fifty years arguing about whether the Aryans invaded. While we argued, recitation lineages that had run continuously since before the Buddha went silent inside two generations, and hardly anyone thought to write down the date.

## The Borrowed Grammar of Reform

This also explains the odd shape of nineteenth-century Hindu reform, and why its arguments never quite fitted the material they were about.

Luther had a move available. Return to the founder's intent, over against the accretions of the institution that claims to represent him. *Sola scriptura* works because there is a scriptura with an origin and a Church demonstrably departed from it. Rammohun Roy, and Dayananda after him, reached for the same move and found no founder to return to. So they substituted a stratum for a person. Back to the Upanishads. Back to the samhitas alone, everything subsequent an accretion.

It was an ingenious improvisation and it was borrowed grammar. Sruti and smriti are two orders of authority with different scopes and an elaborate machinery governing their relation. They are not an early pure text and a late corrupt one, and the reformers had to set that machinery aside in order to make the Protestant argument run at all. Dayananda's insistence that the samhitas contain no image worship and no puranic material is a historical claim of precisely the kind the tradition had never needed to advance, offered in precisely the register of the missionaries he was answering. The Arya Samaj did enormous and necessary work. Its argumentative form was handed to it by its opponents.

That grammar is still running. It assumes a pure text, a corrupted institution and a restoration, and it produces with great reliability a Hinduism that resembles a Protestant denomination: creedal, textualist, embarrassed by ritual, anxious to demonstrate its rationality before an examiner who left the room a long time ago.

## The Authorless Sentence Returns

We are now living inside a strange rhyme.

For the first time since Gutenberg, language is being produced at scale with no author. Every institution modernity built on authorship is registering the shock: copyright, the byline, the degree, the citation, the entire apparatus that certifies a proposition by certifying the person standing behind it. The reflex response is to treat authorlessness as a defect. If nobody is answerable for the sentence, the sentence cannot be trusted.

Jaimini's school spent a millennium arguing the reverse, that a sentence with no author can carry more authority than one with an author, because the authored sentence inherits every limitation of the person and the unauthored one inherits nothing.

I want to be exact about what that does and does not imply. A Mimamsaka would rule against machine-generated text immediately, on his own criteria and without much deliberation. The output is not apauruṣeya. It has an origin in intention, in an enormous number of intentions statistically compressed, which is worse rather than better, since the defects of many speakers are aggregated where a single speaker's could at least be examined. And its transmission is generative rather than exact, which fails the test the tradition cares about above all others. The vikṛti recitations exist to guarantee that nothing is added. A language model exists to add.

The point is that we have no vocabulary of our own for drawing that distinction cleanly. The question of whether unauthored language can bear authority, under what conditions, and what the relation between word and meaning would have to be for it to do so, was worked over with formidable rigour in Sanskrit across a thousand years by people who had no other pressing use for their intellects. Almost nobody now theorising about machine language can read a line of it. They are rediscovering in public, and badly, the outline of a question that Śabara had already sharpened and Kumārila had already defended against the best opposition of his age.

We hold the deepest inquiry ever conducted into what an unauthored sentence can mean, and the most exacting machinery ever devised for carrying a sentence unchanged across three thousand years. We are losing the second while the first sits unread. The seers were never in danger. No philologist could have reached them. It is the reciters we are burying.
