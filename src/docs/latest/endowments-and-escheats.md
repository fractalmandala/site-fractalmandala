# Endowments and Escheats

*The 1817 regulation that first put Hindu temples under state management announced its own logic in its title. The republic kept the logic and dropped the word.*

The first law the East India Company wrote about Indian temples was called the Madras Endowments and Escheats Regulation. Regulation VII of 1817. Its long title says that it provides for the due appropriation of the rents and produce of lands granted for the support of mosques, Hindu temples and colleges, for the maintenance of bridges and choultries, and for the custody and disposal of escheats.

Read that list again. Temple lands, roads, and property with no owner, all in one instrument.

An escheat is the doctrine by which property whose owner cannot be established reverts to the sovereign. It is feudal, Latin in its bones, and perfectly precise about its own assumption: that the world is exhaustively divided among titled owners, and that any gap in that division belongs to the Crown by default. Bona vacantia. Vacant goods. The law does not permit a thing to be held by nobody, so where it finds no holder it supplies one, and the one it supplies is itself.

Nobody sat in Fort St. George and decided that Hindu sacred property was ownerless. The clerk who drafted the Regulation was solving a filing problem. He had lands producing revenue, disbursements he could not trace to a proprietor, and a Board of Revenue that wanted the columns to add up. He put temple endowments and escheats in the same instrument because in the only jurisprudence he possessed they raised the same question, and the question was: whose is this?

He never got an answer he could use. Two centuries later, neither have we.

## The question that had no answer

Walk into the arrangement he was surveying and ask his question of it.

A temple in the Carnatic in 1817 had a deity, and the deity had a place. It had archakas whose right to serve was hereditary and whose families had held the office through more dynasties than the Company had years in the country. It had dharmakartas, managers, sometimes a lineage of them, sometimes a rotation among households. It had a matha attached, or an acarya-parampara that certified the ritual. It had the assembly of the town, the trade bodies, the cultivators of the temple's fields, the musicians, the garland-makers, the men who pulled the ratha. It had a king above all of this whose duty was protection, whose title was the deity's servant, and whose great-grandfather may have made the original grant.

What it did not have was an owner. It had no bishop, no synod, no board of directors holding title in perpetuity in its own name, no charter naming a body corporate. Every single person in the arrangement held their position as a form of service. The word for the man in charge was sevaka. In Bengal the courts would later borrow the local term shebait and it means the same thing: the one who serves. The god holds. Men attend.

The Indic legal imagination arrived at this deliberately and has held it consistently for as long as we have records of it. Dharma has no legislator. It is not enacted by anybody, and so nobody may repeal it, and so no institution can hold the deed to it. What men have with respect to dharma is adhikara: qualification, competence, the earned and graded fitness to perform a particular office. Adhikara is distributed, it is plural, it is answerable to acara, and it never consolidates into title. The archaka's adhikara at the sanctum does not make him the owner of the sanctum. It makes him responsible to it.

##### The tradition's arrangement for its most valuable property was to make sure no one could own it. A jurisprudence built on the premise that everything must be owned by someone will read this as an absence and will fill it.

The fractal holds all the way down. The jiva does not own the atman it carries; it is carried by it. The householder does not own the dharma he keeps; he is kept by it. The community does not own the shrine it maintains; it is constituted by maintaining it. This is one idea repeated at every scale, and the temple is simply where it becomes visible as property law.

## The god who was made a minor

The English courts had to do something. They could not process an arrangement with no title-holder, and litigation kept arriving in which somebody had to be the plaintiff.

So they manufactured one. In 1925 the Privy Council decided Pramatha Nath Mullick v. Pradyumna Kumar Mullick and held that a Hindu idol is a juristic entity with a juridical status, able to sue and be sued, and that the deity's interests are attended to by the shebait, who stands in law with the powers of a manager of the estate of an infant heir.

The analogy is the whole colonial theory of sovereignty in one clause, and the Privy Council chose it without appearing to notice.

The god is a minor. Not incapable, exactly, and certainly not fictional; the court is quite serious that the deity is a person with interests of its own. The deity is simply not *competent*. Its property is real, its rights are real, and its capacity to exercise them must be supplied from outside. And it is a minor of a peculiar kind, because there is no eighteenth birthday in the doctrine, no procedure by which the ward attains majority and takes possession. The guardianship is permanent by construction.

This is precisely the argument that justified the Company's presence on the subcontinent, applied now to the objects of worship. The territory has inhabitants and the inhabitants have rights, and the exercise of those rights requires a competence they do not possess, which the sovereign will therefore exercise on their behalf, permanently, and in their interest. Terra nullius never required that the land be empty. It required only that the law find nobody on it whom it recognised as a proprietor. Pramatha Nath Mullick does the same work on the sacred: the temple is not ownerless, it is ownerless in English, and English is the language the file is kept in.

## What the republic inherited

The convenient thing to believe is that 1947 ended this. The Company's regulation gave way to the Religious Endowments Act of 1863, which handed management to Indian committees, and then in the 1920s Madras built a permanent statutory board, and then the free Indian state passed the Tamil Nadu Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Act of 1959, and the commissioners have been there ever since. The instruments changed hands. The doctrine did not change at all.

It got constitutionalised. Article 25(2)(b) reserves to the state the power to legislate for social welfare and reform and for throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character, with an explanation clarifying that Hindus here includes Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists. Article 26 protects the right of a religious denomination to manage its own affairs in matters of religion, to own property, and to administer it.

Everything turns on the word denomination, and in 1954 the Supreme Court told us what it means. In Shirur Mutt the Court reached for the Oxford Dictionary and produced a test that S. P. Mittal would later restate cleanly: a religious denomination requires a common faith, a common organisation, and a distinctive name.

Consider what that test rewards and what it punishes. The Ramakrishna Mission clears it. It has a name, a governing structure, an identifiable membership. The Sri Aurobindo Society did not clear it, and lost. And a Devi shrine in a Telangana village, maintained across six generations by four families, worshipped by everyone in the district irrespective of sect, belonging to a lineage of service and to no organisation whatsoever, bearing no distinctive name because it never needed to distinguish itself from anything, cannot possibly clear it. Its universality within the tradition is the reason it fails. The test is built to recognise sects. The tradition's centre of gravity has never been sectarian, and so the tradition's centre of gravity is invisible to the test.

Property whose owner the law cannot identify reverts to the sovereign. The Regulation of 1817 said so in its title. The Constitution of India says so through a dictionary definition of a Protestant word.

Shirur Mutt did one more thing, and it is the more remarkable of the two. It held that what constitutes an essential part of a religion is to be determined with reference to the doctrines of that religion, by the Court. A civilization that had gone three thousand years without a magisterium was issued one, seated in Delhi, staffed by judges, and equipped with appellate jurisdiction over the meaning of its own rites. Every invader of the last millennium searched for the Hindu pope in order to negotiate with him or to break him, and could not find the office. The republic did not find it either. It filled it.

## Defined by subtraction

The escheat logic surfaces once more, in the register of personal law, and here it is not even disguised.

Section 2 of the Hindu Marriage Act of 1955 sets out who the Act applies to. Hindus in any of their forms, including Virashaiva, Lingayat, Brahmo, Prarthana and Arya Samaj. Buddhists, Jains and Sikhs. And then clause (c): any other person domiciled in the territories to which this Act extends who is not a Muslim, Christian, Parsi or Jew by religion.

In the statute book of independent India, Hindu is a residuary category. It is what remains when the identifiable claimants have been counted out. Any conveyancer will tell you what that is. It is the residuary estate, the clause at the end of the will that catches whatever the specific bequests failed to name, and it exists because a legal system cannot tolerate a thing belonging to nobody. The named communities came with a corporate form the law could see and address. The rest defaults to the residual jurisdiction, which is to say to the state.

From there the asymmetries follow with no malice required anywhere in the chain. Article 30(1) gives minorities the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice, and in Pramati in 2014 the Supreme Court held that the Right to Education Act cannot be applied to minority educational institutions, aided or unaided. A Hindu school carries the quota and the regulation. A minority school does not. A church has canon law, a diocese, a bishop, a magisterium; the law can hand over the keys and know exactly whose hand it has put them in. A temple has an acarya-parampara the law cannot read, a community of adhikara it cannot enumerate, and a deity it can only treat as a ward.

## The trap in the obvious answer

The instinctive remedy is incorporation. If the law recognises corporate persons, then let us build one. A central Hindu authority, a registry of temples, a doctrinal body, an apex with standing. Give the courts the counterparty they keep asking for and the asymmetry collapses.

This would win the litigation and lose the thing being litigated over.

What the law cannot see is exactly what has kept the tradition alive. Sanatana Dharma has no single point of failure, and that is architecture rather than accident. The Shaiva Siddhanta of one district and the next differ, and neither requires the other's licence. A sampradaya can go dormant for two centuries in one region and be carried forward in another without any body having authorised the transfer. Acara varies by place, lineage and family, and is supposed to. Adhikara is earned in the doing and certified by those who can see the doing, which means the certifying authority is always local and always many. Every conqueror for a thousand years went looking for the head to cut off and found a hydra with no neck. It would be a strange conclusion to that thousand years to grow one ourselves, on the advice of the Registrar of Societies, in order to be legible to a court.

The demand is for a jurisprudence that can hold property without an owner, because that is what the property in question actually is. No church is required to make that case.

## The prior question

The case is live. In May of this year the Supreme Court recalled its order of 2025 that had sent the challenges to the endowments legislation back down to the High Courts, and agreed to hear the constitutional question itself, across Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Puducherry. The Madras High Court has held this year that executive officers of the department cannot interfere with religious practice and custom, and that administration vests in the board of trustees. The Kerala High Court has read the power of superintendence under the endowments statute as supervisory, presupposing a distinct authority that it supervises rather than replaces.

All of this is real progress and all of it is fought inside the frame. The arguments concern the extent of superintendence, the boundary between property and practice, the propriety of one commissioner's order. They accept that a superintendent exists and argue about his reach. None of them puts the prior question, which is how the deity came to be a perpetual minor in the first place, and by what warrant the guardianship passed from the Crown to the Republic without anyone examining the instrument of transfer.

Seventy-nine years is long enough to have produced a category of our own. Indian law has had to invent when it needed to before; it built a whole jurisprudence of the deity's legal personality out of nothing, in a hurry, because litigation demanded it. It can build the harder thing. A form of holding in which the office is service, the competence is graded, the jurisdiction is local and plural, and the title vests nowhere at all, is not beyond the drafting capacity of a country that has been operating exactly that arrangement continuously since before the Company arrived.

Until then the file stays open where the Board of Revenue left it. A ward with property and no capacity, a guardian with capacity and no end date, and a column in a survey where a clerk who could not find the owner wrote the only word his law gave him.

The infant heir is two hundred years old. Nobody has proposed a birthday.
